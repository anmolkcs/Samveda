How a verse becomes a chant

A sāman is not a melody you can improvise. The gāna books fix, for each sāman, which of the seven sāma svaras each syllable takes and how long it is held. So a faithful system has to do three things: get the words right, get the notes right, and be clear about which of those it actually knows.

1 · Text frontend

Any supported script is converted to Devanāgarī internally, then segmented into akṣaras. Two Sanskrit-specific traps are avoided on purpose: Hindi-style schwa deletion (silently turning rāma into rām) never runs, and Vedic accents are treated as combining marks that belong to a syllable rather than punctuation, so अ॒ग्निमी॑ळे stays one word.

2 · Sāma-vikāra engine

Turning a ṛc into a sāman means editing the words in six documented ways: vikāra (substitution), viśleṣa (breaking sandhi), vikarṣaṇa (prolongation), abhyāsa (repetition), virāma (pause) and stobha (inserting syllables like hāu, ho, hā'u). The engine applies these, and the stobha inventory changes with the śākhā — which is why the school selector changes what you hear.

3 · Svara plan

The seven sāma svaras — kṛṣṭa, prathama, dvitīya, tṛtīya, caturtha, mandra, atisvārya — descend from the highest, and Nāradīya Śikṣā relates them to the flute as Ma, Ga, Ri, Sa, Ni, Dha, Pa. They are mapped to semitones so the acoustic model has one note space whether the source is a gāna text, an accented saṃhitā, or a plain verse.

4 · Acoustic model

F0 is not predicted by a network. The svara sequence is given by the tradition and the melody is computed exactly; a network would only add drift. What a network does learn is the phonetics: per-phone durations and the formant tracks (F1/F2/F3) of sustained vowels and consonants. That split is why the model is small enough to train on a free Kaggle T4 — the music-side entropy is low and almost all the difficulty is in the sounds.

Two engines render it. The formant engine is a Klatt-style source-filter cascade with a jittered Rosenberg glottal source, per-phone spectral shaping, formant transitions across consonant boundaries, a nasal antiformant and a small hall. The neural engine keeps the same cascade and takes its formant tracks and durations from a trained checkpoint. Compare them on the same verse — that is what the engine switch is for.

What the modes mean

gānaThe text is a real gāna text with svara numerals. Nothing is invented.
saṃhitāThe text has Vedic accents. The three-tone accent map is honoured: faithful recitation, not gāna.
styleNo notation. The documented vikāras are applied and the result is laid on a gāna skeleton: the right phonetics and musical grammar, but not the sāman this ṛc's own śākhā would sing.

That last distinction is the whole point. A tool that claimed to know the right tune for arbitrary Sanskrit would be lying; one that says which mode it is in lets you rely on it where it is reliable.

Training data — what exists, and what may be used

Text is the easy half: the gāna books are open and enormous. Audio with a transcript is the bottleneck, and much of what exists is not licensed for training. This table says which is which, so a released model never quietly rests on someone else's recording.

Getting to five useful hours

Five hours of purpose-recorded, consented, single-singer Sāma recitation will beat every public source on that list for training a first model — that is the practical lesson from comparable systems. The recording protocol, the akṣara-level alignment scheme and the quality tiering are in docs/DATA.md; the notebook notebooks/kaggle_samadhvani.ipynb runs the whole pipeline on a free T4.
